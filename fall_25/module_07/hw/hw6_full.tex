\subsubsection*{Question 1 (a)}
\begin{flushleft}
    Consider the spherical Gaussian $N(0, I_d)$ in $d$-dimensional space. 
    What is the maximum density achieved by this distribution? At what point is it achieved? 
\end{flushleft}

\subsubsection*{Solution 1 (a)}
The probability density function (PDF) for a $d$-dimensional multivariate Gaussian distribution $N(\mu, \Sigma)$ is:
$$ f(x) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2} |\Sigma|^{1/2}} \exp\left( -\frac{1}{2} (x - \mu)^\top \Sigma^{-1} (x - \mu) \right) $$
Given a spherical Gaussian $N(0, I_d)$:
$$\mu = 0 \quad \text{ and }\quad \Sigma = I_d \rightarrow (\text{definition: }N(\mu, \Sigma))$$
The determinant of any identity matrix is $1$, and inverse of an identity matrix is itself:
$$|\Sigma| = |I_d| = 1 \quad \text{ and }\quad \Sigma^{-1} = I_d^{-1} = I_d$$
Now, substitute $\mu$, $\Sigma$, and $|\Sigma|$ into the PDF formula:
\begin{align*}
    f(x) &= \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2} |\Sigma|^{1/2}} \exp\left( -\frac{1}{2} (x - \mu)^\top \Sigma^{-1} (x - \mu) \right) \\
    &= \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2} (1)^{1/2}} \exp\left( -\frac{1}{2} (x - \mathbf{0})^\top I_d (x - \mathbf{0}) \right) \\
    &= \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}} \exp\left( -\frac{1}{2} x^\top x \right) \\
    &= (2\pi)^{-d/2} \exp\left( -\frac{1}{2} \|x\|^2 \right)
\end{align*}
Next, simplify the expression by taking natural log such that $L(x)=\ln(f(x))$:
\begin{align*}
    L(x) &= \ln\left((2\pi)^{-d/2} \exp\left( -\frac{1}{2} \|x\|^2 \right)\right) \\
    &= \ln\left((2\pi)^{-d/2}\right) + \ln\left(\exp\left( -\frac{1}{2} \|x\|^2 \right)\right) \\
    &= \ln\left((2\pi)^{-d/2}\right) - \frac{1}{2} \|x\|^2  
\end{align*}
By inspection the maximum of $L(x)$ is achived at the mean, $x = 0$.
Hence, the maximum density is:
$$(2\pi)^{-d/2}$$

\subsubsection*{\normalfont}{$\therefore$ The maximum density for a spherical Gaussian $N(0, I_d)$ is $(2\pi)^{-d/2}$, and the point is achieved at $x = 0$}

\subsubsection*{Graduate Level Explanation}
The maximum of any multivariate Gaussian PDF $N(\mu, \Sigma)$ occurs at its mean $\mu$. This is because the exponent is governed by the negative Mahalanobis distance, $-(x-\mu)^\top \Sigma^{-1} (x-\mu)$, which is a positive semi-definite quadratic form. This form is uniquely minimized (at a value of 0) when $x=\mu$. For the standard normal $N(0, I_d)$, the Mahalanobis distance simplifies to the squared Euclidean norm $\|x\|^2$, and the normalization constant $(2\pi)^{-d/2} |\Sigma|^{-1/2}$ simplifies to $(2\pi)^{-d/2}$, which is the value of the density at the mode.

\subsubsection*{Learn Fast Explanation}
Think of a bell curve. It's always tallest at its center (the mean). For the $N(0, I_d)$ distribution, the center is just the origin (the zero vector). The density formula is essentially $\text{Constant} \times \exp(-\text{distance from center})$. To get the biggest value, you want the $\exp(\cdot)$ part to be as large as possible. Since the exponent is negative, you make it largest (it becomes $\exp(0)=1$) by making the "distance from center" zero. This leaves just the constant part, $(2\pi)^{-d/2}$, as the peak height.

\subsubsection*{Question 1 (b)}
\begin{flushleft}
    Consider the spherical Gaussian $N(0, I_d)$ in $d$-dimensional space. 
    In very rough terms, what happens to the maximum density as $d$ grows? 
    (This helps explain why for high-dimensional data we typically work with the logarithm of the density, in order to avoid numerical underflow errors.) 
\end{flushleft}

\subsubsection*{Solution 1 (b)}
From part (a), the maximum density is $f_{\text{max}} = (2\pi)^{-d/2}$, which we can rewrite as:
$$ f_{\text{max}} = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}} = \left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \right)^d $$
The base of this exponent, $\sqrt{2\pi}$, is approximately $\sqrt{6.28} \approx 2.5$, which is a number greater than 1.
\begin{itemize}
    \item When $d=1$, $f_{\text{max}} \approx \frac{1}{2.5} \approx 0.4$
    \item When $d=2$, $f_{\text{max}} \approx \frac{1}{6.28} \approx 0.16$
    \item When $d=10$, $f_{\text{max}} \approx (0.4)^{10} \approx 0.0001$
\end{itemize}
As $d$ (the dimension) grows, the exponent $d$ increases, causing the denominator $(2\pi)^{d/2}$ to grow exponentially.
Consequently, the maximum density $f_{\text{max}}$ approaches $0$ exponentially fast.

This is precisely why we work with log-density. The maximum log-density is:
$$ \log(f_{\text{max}}) = \log\left( (2\pi)^{-d/2} \right) = -\frac{d}{2} \log(2\pi) $$
This value decreases linearly as $d$ grows (e.g., becomes a large negative number) and is numerically stable, whereas the raw density $f_{\text{max}}$ becomes so small it is rounded to $0$ by standard floating-point arithmetic (an underflow error).

\subsubsection*{\normalfont}{$\therefore$ As $d$ grows, the maximum density approaches $0$ exponentially fast. This causes numerical underflow, forcing the use of log-density.}

\subsubsection*{Graduate Level Explanation}
The max density $f_{\text{max}} = (2\pi)^{-d/2}$ decays exponentially as $d$ increases. This phenomenon is a component of the "curse of dimensionality," where the high-dimensional space becomes so vast that the probability mass becomes vanishingly sparse, even at the mode of the distribution. The log-density, $\log(f_{\text{max}}) = -\frac{d}{2}\log(2\pi)$, decreases linearly, which is why all practical high-dimensional probabilistic models perform calculations in log-space to maintain numerical stability and avoid underflow.

\subsubsection*{Learn Fast Explanation}
Imagine you have one pound of butter (your total probability, which is 1) and you have to spread it evenly on a piece of bread.
\begin{itemize}
    \item In 1D, you spread it on a line. The "peak" thickness is $0.4$.
    \item In 2D, you spread it on a square. The peak thickness is already down to $0.16$.
    \item In 100D, you are spreading that same pound of butter over an impossibly huge 100-dimensional space.
\end{itemize}
The butter (probability) gets spread so thin that even the thickest point (the mean) has a density that is basically zero. This number becomes too small for a computer to store, so we use logarithms to work with it instead.

\subsubsection*{Question 2 (a)}
\begin{flushleft}
    \textbf{Length concentration.} Write a procedure that draws 10000 points $X_1, \ldots, X_{10000}$ from the spherical Gaussian $N(0, I_d)$ and plots a histogram of their (squared) lengths $\|X_i\|^2$. 
    Do this for $d=10$. What seems to be the typical (squared) length? 
\end{flushleft}

\subsubsection*{Solution 2 (a)}

\subsubsection*{Python Code: d=10}
\begin{lstlisting}
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# set dimension and number of samples
d = 10
num_samples = 10000
np.random.seed(42) 

# get 10000 points from N(0, I_d)
samples = np.random.normal(0, 1, size=(num_samples, d))

# calc the squared L2 norm for each point
squared_lengths_d10 = np.sum(samples**2, axis=1)

# calculate mean
sample_mean_d10 = np.mean(squared_lengths_d10)
print(f"Dimension (d): {d}")
print(f"Sample Mean of ||X||^2: {sample_mean_d10:.4f}")

# plot the histogram
plt.figure(figsize=(10, 6))
plt.hist(squared_lengths_d10, bins=50, color='skyblue', edgecolor='black', alpha=0.7, density=True,label=f'Histogram of {num_samples} samples')
plt.axvline(d, color='red', linestyle='--', linewidth=2, 
            label=f'Theoretical Mean (d = {d}): $E[||X||^2]$')
plt.axvline(sample_mean_d10, color='blue', linestyle=':', linewidth=2, 
            label=f'Sample Mean = {sample_mean_d10:.4f}')
# set plot args and save
plt.title('Histogram of Squared Lengths for $N(0, I_{10})$', fontsize=14)
plt.xlabel('Squared Length $||X||^2$', fontsize=12)
plt.ylabel('Density', fontsize=12)
plt.legend()
plt.grid(True, linestyle='--', alpha=0.5)
plt.savefig('histogram_d10.png', dpi=150, bbox_inches='tight')
\end{lstlisting}

\subsubsection*{Calculate Concentration}
To determine if the distribution is more concentrated, we must compare its relative spread. This is measured using the coefficient of variation or $CV$, which is defined as:
$$CV = \frac{\sigma}{\mu} \quad \text{ where } \quad \mu=d \text{ ,  } \sigma = \sqrt{2d}$$
For, $d=10$ and $d=100$, the coefficient of variation is:
$$CV_{10} = \frac{\sqrt{20}}{10} \approx 0.447\quad \text{ and }\quad CV_{100} = \frac{\sqrt{200}}{100} \approx 0.141$$
Hence, $CV_{100} < CV_{10}$ 

\subsubsection*{\normalfont}{$\therefore$ the distribution for $d=100$ is more concentrated than the distribution for $d=10$}

\subsubsection*{Graduate Level Explanation}
This demonstrates the phenomenon of **length concentration** in high dimensions. The coefficient of variation (CV) for the $\chi^2(d)$ distribution is $\text{CV} = \frac{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(Y)}}{\mathbb{E}[Y]} = \frac{\sqrt{2d}}{d} = \sqrt{\frac{2}{d}}$. This value scales as $O(1/\sqrt{d})$, meaning it approaches 0 as $d \to \infty$. Consequently, in high-dimensional spaces, the squared lengths of $N(0, I_d)$ vectors are not broadly distributed but instead concentrate in a "thin shell" very close to the expected value $d$.

\subsubsection*{Learn Fast Explanation}
Welcome to the "curse of dimensionality." On our 10-D dartboard, the squared distances were spread out (from $\approx 0$ to $25$). On the 100-D dartboard, the squared distances are centered at 100, but they are *packed* in a much tighter band (e.g., almost all are between 80 and 120). Relative to its mean of 100, this band is very narrow. This means in high dimensions, *all* "typical" points are about the same distance from the center—a very counter-intuitive but fundamental concept.

\subsection*{Solution 3}
\noindent\rule{\textwidth}{0.4pt}\\
\subsubsection*{Question 3}
\begin{flushleft}
    Suppose $X \in \mathbb{R}^2$ has a Gaussian distribution with mean $\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 2\end{bmatrix}$ and covariance matrix $\begin{bmatrix}2 & 0 \\ 0 & 4\end{bmatrix}$. 
    What is the distribution of the projection of $X$ onto direction $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$? 
\end{flushleft}

\subsubsection*{Solution 3}
Let $X \sim N(\mu, \Sigma)$ where:
$$ \mu = \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 2\end{bmatrix} \quad \quad \Sigma = \begin{bmatrix}2 & 0 \\ 0 & 4\end{bmatrix} \quad \quad v = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 1\end{bmatrix}$$

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Calculate the mean $\mu_Y$:
    \begin{align*}
    \mu_Y &= v^\top\mu \\
          &= \begin{bmatrix}1/\sqrt{2} & 1/\sqrt{2}\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 2\end{bmatrix} \\
          &= \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \cdot 1\right) + \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \cdot 2\right) \\
          &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} + \frac{2}{\sqrt{2}} \\
          &= \frac{3}{\sqrt{2}}
    \end{align*}
    \item Calculate the variance $\sigma_Y^2$:
    \begin{align*}
        \sigma_Y^2 &= v^\top\Sigma v \\
        &= \begin{bmatrix}1/\sqrt{2} & 1/\sqrt{2}\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}2 & 0 \\ 0 & 4\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}1/\sqrt{2} \\ 1/\sqrt{2}\end{bmatrix} \\
        &= \begin{bmatrix}1/\sqrt{2} & 1/\sqrt{2}\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 2 \cdot (1/\sqrt{2}) + 0 \\ 0 + 4 \cdot (1/\sqrt{2}) \end{bmatrix} \\
        &= \begin{bmatrix}1/\sqrt{2} & 1/\sqrt{2}\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 2/\sqrt{2} \\ 4/\sqrt{2} \end{bmatrix} \\
        &= \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \cdot \frac{2}{\sqrt{2}}\right) + \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \cdot \frac{4}{\sqrt{2}}\right) \\
        &= \frac{2}{2} + \frac{4}{2} \\
        &= 1 + 2 = 3
    \end{align*}
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection*{\normalfont}{$\therefore$ The distribution of the projection is $N\left(\frac{3}{\sqrt{2}}, 3\right)$.}
\subsubsection*{Graduate Level Explanation}
This result is a direct consequence of the affine transformation property of Gaussian vectors. If $X \sim N(\mu, \Sigma)$ and $Y = AX + b$, then $Y \sim N(A\mu + b, A\Sigma A^\top)$. In this problem, $A = v^\top$ and $b = 0$. This operation finds the 1-dimensional marginal distribution of the random vector $X$ along the axis defined by the vector $v$. Since the original covariance $\Sigma$ is diagonal, $X_1$ and $X_2$ are independent, but the projection $Y = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(X_1 + X_2)$ is a sum of these independent variables, and its variance is the sum of their scaled variances.

\subsubsection*{Learn Fast Explanation}
Think of your 2D Gaussian $X$ as an oval-shaped cloud of 1,000s of data points, centered at $(1, 2)$. The oval is twice as wide in the $y$-direction (variance 4) as it is in the $x$-direction (variance 2). The "direction $v$" is just the 45-degree line $y=x$. Projecting the cloud onto this line is like shining a flashlight from behind the cloud and casting a shadow onto that 45-degree line. That shadow will also be a "cloud" of points, but it's 1-dimensional, so it's just a simple bell curve. Our calculation found the properties of that shadow-cloud: its center (mean) is at $3/\sqrt{2}$ ($\approx 2.12$) and its "spread" (variance) is $3$.

\subsection*{Solution 4}
\noindent\rule{\textwidth}{0.4pt}\\
\subsubsection*{Question 4 (a)}
\begin{flushleft}
    We have three sensors $(X_1, X_2, X_3)$ whose joint distribution is Gaussian with mean zero and covariance matrix 
    \[
    \begin{bmatrix}
    6 & 3 & 1 \\
    3 & 9 & 0 \\
    1 & 0 & 2
    \end{bmatrix}
    \]
    What is the distribution of sensor $X_3$? 
\end{flushleft}

\subsubsection*{Solution 4 (a)}
A key property of the multivariate Gaussian distribution is that any marginal distribution is also Gaussian. To find the distribution of a subset of variables, we simply extract the corresponding entries from the mean vector and the covariance matrix.

Let $X = \begin{bmatrix} X_1 \\ X_2 \\ X_3 \end{bmatrix} \sim N(\mu, \Sigma)$, where:
$$ \mu = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \quad \text{and} \quad \Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} 6 & 3 & 1 \\ 3 & 9 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix} $$
The distribution for $X_3$ is a 1D Gaussian $N(\mu_3, \sigma_3^2)$.
\begin{itemize}
    \item The mean $\mu_3$ is the 3rd element of the mean vector $\mu$, which is $0$.
    \item The variance $\sigma_3^2$ is the (3, 3) element of the covariance matrix $\Sigma$, which is $2$.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{\normalfont}{$\therefore$ The distribution of sensor $X_3$ is $N(0, 2)$.}

\subsubsection*{Graduate Level Explanation}
Multivariate Gaussian distributions are closed under marginalization. For a jointly Gaussian vector $X \sim N(\mu, \Sigma)$, the marginal distribution of any sub-vector $X_a$ is $N(\mu_a, \Sigma_{aa})$, where $\mu_a$ and $\Sigma_{aa}$ are the sub-vector and sub-matrix of $\mu$ and $\Sigma$ corresponding to the variables in $X_a$. Here, $a=\{3\}$, $\mu_a = \mu_3 = 0$, and $\Sigma_{aa} = \Sigma_{33} = 2$.

\subsubsection*{Learn Fast Explanation}
Think of the big covariance matrix as a "lookup table" for the properties of all sensors. If you only want to know about $X_3$, you just look up its personal info. The mean for $X_3$ is the 3rd number in the mean vector (which is 0). The variance for $X_3$ is the 3rd-row, 3rd-column entry in the covariance matrix (which is 2). Therefore, $X_3$ is just a simple bell curve centered at 0 with a variance of 2.

\subsubsection*{Solution 4 (b)}

We are interested in the joint distribution of $X_a = \begin{bmatrix} X_1 \\ X_2 \end{bmatrix}$.
\begin{itemize}
    \item The mean vector $\mu_a$ corresponds to the 1st and 2nd elements of $\mu$:
    $$ \mu_a = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} $$
    \item The covariance matrix $\Sigma_{aa}$ corresponds to the 1st and 2nd rows and columns of $\Sigma$:
    $$ \Sigma_{aa} = \begin{bmatrix} 6 & 3 \\ 3 & 9 \end{bmatrix} $$
\end{itemize}
The resulting distribution is a 2-dimensional Gaussian.

\subsubsection*{\normalfont}{$\therefore$ The joint distribution of $X_1, X_2$ is $N\left(\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 6 & 3 \\ 3 & 9 \end{bmatrix}\right)$.}

\subsubsection*{Graduate Level Explanation}
This again uses the closure-under-marginalization property. The $2 \times 2$ principal sub-matrix $\Sigma_{aa}$ is itself a valid covariance matrix (symmetric and positive definite), and the corresponding sub-vector $\mu_a$ is the mean. This property is what allows us to analyze subsets of variables from a large joint model without re-deriving their distributions.

\subsubsection*{Learn Fast Explanation}
Just like in part (a), we're using the big matrix as a "lookup table." This time, we want to know about the *team* $(X_1, X_2)$. So, we grab their team info: their means (the 1st and 2nd entries of $\mu$) and their covariance block (the $2 \times 2$ block in the top-left corner of $\Sigma$). This block tells us $X_1$'s variance (6), $X_2$'s variance (9), and how they vary together (their covariance, 3).

\subsection*{Solution 4}
\noindent\rule{\textwidth}{0.4pt}\\
\subsubsection*{Question 4 (c)}
\begin{flushleft}
    We have three sensors $(X_1, X_2, X_3)$ whose joint distribution is Gaussian with mean zero and covariance matrix 
    \[
    \begin{bmatrix}
    6 & 3 & 1 \\
    3 & 9 & 0 \\
    1 & 0 & 2
    \end{bmatrix}
    \]
    Suppose we observe $X_2=2$ and $X_3=1$. 
    What is the resulting conditional distribution of $X_1$? 
\end{flushleft}

\subsubsection*{Solution 4 (c)}
We need to find the conditional distribution of $X_1 \mid (X_2, X_3)$.
Let $X_a = X_1$ and $X_b = \begin{bmatrix} X_2 \\ X_3 \end{bmatrix}$. We observe $x_b = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$.
We partition the mean vector $\mu$ and covariance matrix $\Sigma$:
$$ \mu = \begin{bmatrix} \mu_a \\ \mu_b \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ \hline 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} $$
$$ \Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} \Sigma_{aa} & \Sigma_{ab} \\ \Sigma_{ba} & \Sigma_{bb} \end{bmatrix} = \left[ \begin{array}{c|cc} 6 & 3 & 1 \\ \hline 3 & 9 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 2 \end{array} \right] $$
From this, we identify the blocks:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\mu_a = 0$
    \item $\mu_b = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$
    \item $\Sigma_{aa} = 6$
    \item $\Sigma_{ab} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$
    \item $\Sigma_{ba} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$
    \item $\Sigma_{bb} = \begin{bmatrix} 9 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$
\end{itemize}
The conditional distribution $X_a \mid X_b = x_b$ is also Gaussian, $N(\mu_{a|b}, \Sigma_{a|b})$, with:
\begin{align*}
    \mu_{a|b} &= \mu_a + \Sigma_{ab} \Sigma_{bb}^{-1} (x_b - \mu_b) \\
    \Sigma_{a|b} &= \Sigma_{aa} - \Sigma_{ab} \Sigma_{bb}^{-1} \Sigma_{ba}
\end{align*}
**1. Compute $\Sigma_{bb}^{-1}$:**
Since $\Sigma_{bb}$ is diagonal, its inverse is the inverse of its diagonal elements:
$$ \Sigma_{bb}^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 9 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1/9 & 0 \\ 0 & 1/2 \end{bmatrix} $$
**2. Compute the conditional mean $\mu_{a|b}$:**
\begin{align*}
    \mu_{a|b} &= 0 + \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1/9 & 0 \\ 0 & 1/2 \end{bmatrix} \left( \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \right) \\
              &= \begin{bmatrix} 3 \cdot \frac{1}{9} & 1 \cdot \frac{1}{2} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \\
              &= \begin{bmatrix} 1/3 & 1/2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \\
              &= \left(\frac{1}{3} \cdot 2\right) + \left(\frac{1}{2} \cdot 1\right) = \frac{2}{3} + \frac{1}{2} \\
              &= \frac{4}{6} + \frac{3}{6} = \frac{7}{6}
\end{align*}
**3. Compute the conditional variance $\Sigma_{a|b}$:**
\begin{align*}
    \Sigma_{a|b} &= 6 - \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1/9 & 0 \\ 0 & 1/2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \\
              &= 6 - \begin{bmatrix} 1/3 & 1/2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \\
              &= 6 - \left( \left(\frac{1}{3} \cdot 3\right) + \left(\frac{1}{2} \cdot 1\right) \right) \\
              &= 6 - (1 + 0.5) = 6 - 1.5 = 4.5
\end{align*}
The conditional variance is $4.5$, or $9/2$.

\subsubsection*{\normalfont}{$\therefore$ The resulting conditional distribution of $X_1$ is $N\left(\frac{7}{6}, \frac{9}{2}\right)$ (or $N(1.167, 4.5)$).}

\subsubsection*{Graduate Level Explanation}
The formulas for the conditional mean and covariance are known as the Schur complement of the block $\Sigma_{aa}$. The conditional covariance $\Sigma_{a|b} = \Sigma_{aa} - \Sigma_{ab} \Sigma_{bb}^{-1} \Sigma_{ba}$ represents the reduction in uncertainty (variance) of $X_a$ after observing $X_b$. It is crucially independent of the observed value $x_b$. The conditional mean $\mu_{a|b}$ is the prior mean $\mu_a$ adjusted by a linear correction term, $\Sigma_{ab} \Sigma_{bb}^{-1} (x_b - \mu_b)$, which accounts for the observed "residual" $(x_b - \mu_b)$ propagated through the covariance structure.

\subsubsection*{Learn Fast Explanation}
Before we knew anything, our best guess for $X_1$ was its mean, 0.
Then, we got data: $X_2=2$ and $X_3=1$.
\begin{itemize}
    \item The matrix shows $X_1$ is positively correlated with $X_2$ (covariance=3) and $X_3$ (covariance=1).
    \item Since both $X_2$ and $X_3$ came in "hot" (they were positive, not 0), we should update our guess for $X_1$ to also be positive. The calculation $\mu_{a|b} = 7/6$ is the precise, weighted update.
    \item Our original uncertainty (variance) about $X_1$ was 6. By getting data from two related sensors, we've *learned* something and become *less uncertain*.
    \item The calculation $\Sigma_{a|b} = 6 - 1.5 = 4.5$ shows our new variance is 4.5, which is smaller than 6, reflecting our gain in knowledge.
\end{itemize}

    What is the probability of the configuration $F_1 = F_2 = F_3 = 1, F_4 = 0$? 
\end{flushleft}
\subsubsection*{Solution 6 (b)}
The joint probability distribution specified by this Bayesian Network factorizes according to the graph structure:
$$ P(F_1, F_2, F_3, F_4) = P(F_1) \cdot P(F_2) \cdot P(F_3 \mid F_1, F_2) \cdot P(F_4 \mid F_3) $$
To find the probability of the specific configuration $F_1=1, F_2=1, F_3=1, F_4=0$, we plug these values into the formula and look up the corresponding probabilities from our CPTs in part (a).
\begin{align*}
    P(F_1=1, F_2=1, F_3=1, F_4=0) &= P(F_1=1) \times P(F_2=1) \times P(F_3=1 \mid F_1=1, F_2=1) \times P(F_4=0 \mid F_3=1) \\
                                 &= (0.5) \times (0.7) \times (0.8) \times (0.1) \\
                                 &= (0.35) \times (0.08) \\
                                 &= 0.028
\end{align*}

\subsubsection*{Verification}
The joint probability formula correctly applies the chain rule of probability as defined by the graph's topology (parents of $F_3$ are $F_1, F_2$; parent of $F_4$ is $F_3$). The values are correctly sourced from the CPTs:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $P(F_1=1) = 0.5$ (from CPT for $F_1$)
    \item $P(F_2=1) = 0.7$ (from CPT for $F_2$)
    \item $P(F_3=1 \mid F_1=1, F_2=1) = 0.8$ (from row 4 of CPT for $F_3$)
    \item $P(F_4=0 \mid F_3=1) = 0.1$ (from row 2 of CPT for $F_4$)
\end{itemize}
The multiplication $0.5 \times 0.7 \times 0.8 \times 0.1 = 0.028$ is correct.

\subsubsection*{\normalfont}{$\therefore$ The probability of the configuration $F_1 = F_2 = F_3 = 1, F_4 = 0$ is $0.028$.}

\subsubsection*{Graduate Level Explanation}
This calculation is a fundamental example of exact inference in a Bayesian network. By leveraging the conditional independencies encoded in the graph, we compute the joint probability using the "chain rule for Bayesian networks." This avoids the need to construct the full joint probability table (which would have $2^4 = 16$ entries), instead only requiring the storage of $2 + 2 + 8 + 4 = 16$ parameters in the CPTs. The query is a simple lookup in the factored representation.

\subsubsection*{Learn Fast Explanation}
To find the probability of this specific story, you just "chain" the events together, multiplying their probabilities.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item What's the chance $F_1$ goes? **0.5**
    \item ...AND what's the chance $F_2$ goes? **0.7**
    \item ...AND, *given that both went*, what's the chance $F_3$ goes? (Look at $F_3$'s rulebook, row 4): **0.8**
    \item ...AND, *given that $F_3$ went*, what's the chance $F_4$ does *not* go? (Look at $F_4$'s rulebook, row 2): **0.1**
\end{enumerate}
Just multiply those numbers together: $0.5 \times 0.7 \times 0.8 \times 0.1 = 0.028$, or 2.8%.

\subsubsection*{Question 7}
\begin{flushleft}
    \textbf{Non-uniqueness of underlying DAG.} Suppose random variables $X$ and $Y$ take values in $\{0,1\}$. 
    Does the joint distribution of $(X, Y)$ necessarily factor over the DAG on the left below? 
    What about the DAG on the right? 

    \begin{center}
    \begin{tikzpicture}[node distance=1.5cm]
        \node[circle, draw] (F1) {$X$}; 
        \node[circle, draw, right=of F1] (F3) {$Y$}; 
        \node[circle, draw, right=of F3] (F2) {$X$};
        \node[circle, draw, right=of F2] (F4) {$Y$};
        \draw[-Stealth] (F1) -- (F3); 
        \draw[-Stealth] (F4) -- (F2); 
    \end{tikzpicture}
    \end{center}
\end{flushleft}

\subsubsection*{Solution 7}
By the fundamental **chain rule of probability**, any joint distribution $P(X, Y)$ can be factored in two ways, without making any assumptions at all:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item $P(X, Y) = P(X) P(Y \mid X)$
    \item $P(X, Y) = P(Y) P(X \mid Y)$
\end{enumerate}
Now let's look at the factorizations implied by the two DAGs:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{DAG on the left ($X \to Y$):} The Bayesian network factorization rule states that the joint probability is the product of each variable's probability conditioned on its parents.
        \begin{itemize}
            \item Parents of $X$: None
            \item Parents of $Y$: $X$
        \end{itemize}
        This implies the factorization $P(X, Y) = P(X) P(Y \mid X)$. As shown in (1), this is always true by the chain rule.
        
    \item \textbf{DAG on the right ($Y \to X$):}
        \begin{itemize}
            \item Parents of $X$: $Y$
            \item Parents of $Y$: None
        \end{itemize}
        This implies the factorization $P(X, Y) = P(Y) P(X \mid Y)$. As shown in (2), this is also always true by the chain rule.
\end{itemize}
Since both factorizations are universally true for any joint distribution $P(X, Y)$, the distribution necessarily factors over both DAGs.

\subsubsection*{\normalfont}{$\therefore$ Yes, any joint distribution $P(X, Y)$ necessarily factors over both the DAG on the left ($X \to Y$) and the DAG on the right ($Y \to X$). Both are simply applications of the chain rule of probability ($P(X,Y) = P(X)P(Y \mid X)$ and $P(X,Y) = P(Y)P(X \mid Y)$, respectively)}

\subsubsection*{Graduate Level Explanation}
The two DAGs, $X \to Y$ and $Y \to X$, are **Markov equivalent**.  They belong to the same equivalence class because they imply the exact same set of conditional independencies (specifically, none, as both graphs are fully connected). Since the chain rule $P(X,Y) = P(X)P(Y \mid X)$ and $P(X,Y) = P(Y)P(X \mid Y)$ are universally true, any joint distribution $P(X,Y)$ can be parameterized by either model. This illustrates that without interventional data (e.g., $do(X)$) or strong prior assumptions, observational data alone is insufficient to distinguish causal direction (i.e., whether $X$ causes $Y$ or $Y$ causes $X$).

\subsubsection*{Learn Fast Explanation}
Think of the chain rule as two ways to tell a story about $X$ and $Y$ happening together.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item You can say: "First, $X$ happened (with probability $P(X)$), and *then* $Y$ happened given $X$ (with probability $P(Y \mid X)$)."
    \item Or you can say: "First, $Y$ happened (with probability $P(Y)$), and *then* $X$ happened given $Y$ (with probability $P(X \mid Y)$)."
\end{enumerate}
Both of these "stories" ($P(X)P(Y \mid X)$ and $P(Y)P(X \mid Y)$) are perfectly valid mathematical ways to describe the *same* final outcome, $P(X, Y)$. The two graphs in the question just represent these two different-but-equal ways of telling the story. Since you can *always* tell the story both ways, *any* joint probability $P(X, Y)$ will fit *both* graphs.

\subsubsection*{Question 8 (b)}
\begin{flushleft}
    \textbf{Genes, diseases, and outcomes.} ... [Intro text from 8(a) omitted for brevity] ...

    What is the probability of disease $(D=1)$? 
\end{flushleft}

\subsubsection*{Solution 8 (b)}
To find the marginal probability $P(D=1)$, we must sum over all possible states of its parents, $G_1$ and $G_2$. This is an application of the Law of Total Probability.
$$ P(D=1) = \sum_{g_1 \in \{0,1\}} \sum_{g_2 \in \{0,1\}} P(G_1=g_1, G_2=g_2, D=1) $$
Using the network's factorization, $P(G_1, G_2, D) = P(G_1)P(G_2)P(D \mid G_1, G_2)$:
\begin{align*}
    P(D=1) &= P(G_1=0)P(G_2=0)P(D=1 \mid G_1=0, G_2=0) \\
           &+ P(G_1=0)P(G_2=1)P(D=1 \mid G_1=0, G_2=1) \\
           &+ P(G_1=1)P(G_2=0)P(D=1 \mid G_1=1, G_2=0) \\
           &+ P(G_1=1)P(G_2=1)P(D=1 \mid G_1=1, G_2=1)
\end{align*}
Now, we plug in the values from our CPTs:
\begin{align*}
    P(D=1) &= (0.9)(0.8)(0.0) + (0.9)(0.2)(0.1) + (0.1)(0.8)(0.2) + (0.1)(0.2)(0.5) \\
           &= 0.0 + 0.018 + 0.016 + 0.010 \\
           &= 0.044
\end{align*}

\subsubsection*{\normalfont}{$\therefore$ The probability of disease $(D=1)$ is $0.044$, or 4.4%.}

\subsubsection*{Graduate Level Explanation}
The calculation of $P(D=1)$ is a standard "sum-product" operation for marginal inference. We compute the marginal probability of a node by summing its joint probability with its parents over all possible states of the parents. This operation, $\sum_{G_1, G_2} P(G_1)P(G_2)P(D=1 \mid G_1, G_2)$, is a form of "belief propagation" from the root nodes $G_1$ and $G_2$ to the child node $D$.

\subsubsection*{Learn Fast Explanation}
To find the total chance of getting the disease, we have to find the chance of *each way* it can happen, and then add those chances up. There are 4 "scenarios" for the genes:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item (Gene 1 normal) AND (Gene 2 normal) AND (Disease): $(0.9 \times 0.8 \times 0.0) = 0.0$
    \item (Gene 1 normal) AND (Gene 2 mutant) AND (Disease): $(0.9 \times 0.2 \times 0.1) = 0.018$
    \item (Gene 1 mutant) AND (Gene 2 normal) AND (Disease): $(0.1 \times 0.8 \times 0.2) = 0.016$
    \item (Gene 1 mutant) AND (Gene 2 mutant) AND (Disease): $(0.1 \times 0.2 \times 0.5) = 0.010$
\end{enumerate}
Add up all these scenarios: $0.0 + 0.018 + 0.016 + 0.010 = 0.044$.

\subsubsection*{Question 8 (c)}

    What is the probability of paralysis $(P=1)$?

\subsubsection*{Solution 8 (c)}
We find the marginal probability $P(P=1)$ by marginalizing over its parent, $D$.
$$ P(P=1) = \sum_{d \in \{0,1\}} P(D=d, P=1) $$
Using the Law of Total Probability and the network factorization:
$$ P(P=1) = P(P=1 \mid D=0)P(D=0) + P(P=1 \mid D=1)P(D=1) $$
From part (b), we know $P(D=1) = 0.044$.
Therefore, $P(D=0) = 1 - P(D=1) = 1 - 0.044 = 0.956$.
Now, we plug in all values from our CPTs and previous calculations:
\begin{align*}
    P(P=1) &= (0.01) \cdot (0.956) + (0.5) \cdot (0.044) \\
           &= 0.00956 + 0.022 \\
           &= 0.03156
\end{align*}

\subsubsection*{\normalfont}{$\therefore$ The probability of paralysis $(P=1)$ is $0.03156$, or $\approx 3.16\%$.}

\subsubsection*{Graduate Level Explanation}
This is another marginal inference computation. The probability $P(D)$ computed in 8(b) acts as the prior belief (or "message") for the node $D$. We then use this belief to compute the marginal for $P$ by applying the chain rule, $P(P) = \sum_D P(P \mid D)P(D)$. This two-step process demonstrates how belief propagates sequentially through the network.

\subsubsection*{Learn Fast Explanation}
What's the total chance of paralysis? Again, we find the chance of *each way* it can happen and add them up.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item (No disease) AND (Paralysis): $P(D=0) \times P(P=1 \mid D=0) = (0.956) \times (0.01) = 0.00956$
    \item (Have disease) AND (Paralysis): $P(D=1) \times P(P=1 \mid D=1) = (0.044) \times (0.5) = 0.022$
\end{enumerate}
Add those two scenarios: $0.00956 + 0.022 = 0.03156$.

\subsubsection*{Question 8 (d)}
\begin{flushleft}
    \textbf{Genes, diseases, and outcomes.} ... [Intro text from 8(a) omitted for brevity] ...

    We observe that a person has paralysis. What is the probability that they have the disease? 
\end{flushleft}

\subsubsection*{Solution 8 (d)}
We need to calculate the conditional probability $P(D=1 \mid P=1)$. We use Bayes' rule:
$$ P(D=1 \mid P=1) = \frac{P(P=1 \mid D=1) P(D=1)}{P(P=1)} $$
We have all three components from our previous calculations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $P(P=1 \mid D=1) = 0.5$ (from the CPT for $P$)
    \item $P(D=1) = 0.044$ (from part 8b)
    \item $P(P=1) = 0.03156$ (from part 8c)
\end{itemize}
Now, plug them in:
\begin{align*}
    P(D=1 \mid P=1) &= \frac{0.5 \times 0.044}{0.03156} \\
                   &= \frac{0.022}{0.03156} \\
                   &\approx 0.69708
\end{align*}

\subsubsection*{\normalfont}{$\therefore$ The probability that a person with paralysis has the disease is $\approx 0.697$ (or 69.7%).}

\subsubsection*{Graduate Level Explanation}
This is a classic example of diagnostic reasoning (abductive inference) in a Bayesian network. We are calculating a posterior probability $P(\text{Cause} \mid \text{Effect})$. The prior probability of disease $P(D=1)$ was only 4.4%, but after observing the "paralysis" evidence, our belief in the "disease" state is significantly updated to 69.7%. The term $P(P=1)$ acts as the marginal likelihood of the evidence.

\subsubsection*{Learn Fast Explanation}
This is Bayes' rule in action. We want $P(\text{Disease} \mid \text{Paralysis})$.
\begin{itemize}
    \item **Numerator:** What's the chance of (Have disease) AND (Paralysis)? We found this in 8(c): $0.022$.
    \item **Denominator:** What's the *total* chance of (Paralysis)? We found this in 8(c): $0.03156$.
\end{itemize}
So, the probability is just $\frac{0.022}{0.03156} \approx 0.697$.
In plain English: "Of all the ways a person could be paralyzed (which is 3.16% of people), what fraction of them got it from the disease (which is 2.2% of people)?" The answer is $\frac{2.2}{3.16}$, which is $\approx 69.7\%$.

\subsubsection*{Solution 8 (e)}
We need to calculate $P(G_1=1 \mid D=1)$. We use Bayes' rule:
$$ P(G_1=1 \mid D=1) = \frac{P(D=1, G_1=1)}{P(D=1)} $$
We have the denominator $P(D=1) = 0.044$ from part (8b).
We must calculate the numerator, which is the joint probability $P(D=1, G_1=1)$. To do this, we marginalize over $G_2$:
\begin{align*}
    P(D=1, G_1=1) &= \sum_{g_2 \in \{0,1\}} P(G_1=1, G_2=g_2, D=1) \\
                 &= P(G_1=1)P(G_2=0)P(D=1 \mid G_1=1, G_2=0) \\
                 &+ P(G_1=1)P(G_2=1)P(D=1 \mid G_1=1, G_2=1)
\end{align*}
Using values from the CPTs:
\begin{align*}
    P(D=1, G_1=1) &= (0.1)(0.8)(0.2) + (0.1)(0.2)(0.5) \\
                 &= 0.016 + 0.010 \\
                 &= 0.026
\end{align*}
Now we can solve Bayes' rule:
\begin{align*}
    P(G_1=1 \mid D=1) &= \frac{0.026}{0.044} = \frac{26}{44} = \frac{13}{22} \\
                   &\approx 0.5909
\end{align*}

\subsubsection*{\normalfont}{$\therefore$ The probability that a person with the disease has the mutant $G_1$ gene is $\frac{13}{22} \approx 0.591$ (or 59.1%).}

\subsubsection*{Graduate Level Explanation}
This is another diagnostic inference, $P(G_1=1 \mid D=1)$. We cannot use the simpler form $P(D=1 \mid G_1=1)P(G_1=1)$ because $P(D=1 \mid G_1=1)$ is not a value in our CPTs, as $D$ also depends on $G_2$. We must first compute the joint $P(D=1, G_1=1)$ by marginalizing out $G_2$. This demonstrates an inference query that "skips" a node in the graph, as we are reasoning from $D$ back to its parent $G_1$.

\subsubsection*{Learn Fast Explanation}
We want $P(\text{Mutant } G_1 \mid \text{Disease})$.
\begin{itemize}
    \item **Denominator:** What's the total chance of having the disease? From 8(b), it's $0.044$.
    \item **Numerator:** What's the chance of (Mutant $G_1$) AND (Disease)? We find this by adding the two ways it can happen:
        1. (Mutant $G_1$) AND (Normal $G_2$) AND (Disease): $(0.1 \times 0.8 \times 0.2) = 0.016$
        2. (Mutant $G_1$) AND (Mutant $G_2$) AND (Disease): $(0.1 \times 0.2 \times 0.5) = 0.010$
        Total for numerator = $0.016 + 0.010 = 0.026$.
\end{itemize}
The answer is $\frac{0.026}{0.044} \approx 0.591$.
The chance of having the mutant gene $G_1$ was only 10%. But *given* you have the disease, that chance jumps to 59.1%.
